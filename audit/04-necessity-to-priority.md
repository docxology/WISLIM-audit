# From necessity to priority

## The claim

The paper moves from a necessity claim to a priority claim:

> "T1 (Foundational Priority): Life protection is a necessary condition for the
> instantiation of any value." (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:3262`–`:3264`)

> "Therefore, protecting life (decision-making capacity) is logically prior to
> protecting any specific value." (`:5324`–`:5326`)

> "whenever life is affected, life takes non-negotiable first priority over all
> non-life objectives." (`:150`–`:154`)

The first is a claim about necessary conditions. The second is a claim about
lexical priority. They are not the same.

## The counterexample

Oxygen is a necessary condition for human value. Without oxygen, no human can
experience, value, judge, prove, or act. Does it follow that oxygen is the
highest-priority constraint in every decision? No. We routinely trade oxygen
risk for other goods: we drive cars, fly planes, build cities, and perform
surgery. We treat oxygen as a background condition, not as a lexically dominant
value.

Gravity is a necessary condition for stable objects. We do not treat gravity as
the supreme decision constraint. Computational stability is necessary for
complex AI systems; we do not treat it as the highest value. Social cooperation
is necessary for many values; we do not treat it as lexically prior to justice
or truth.

The paper might say life is special because it is the *subject* of value, not
just a condition. That is a stronger premise. But it still does not yield
lexical priority. A subject of value can choose to risk their life for a value
they hold higher: a parent for a child, a soldier for a country, a dissident for
freedom, a patient for dignity. The paper's framework must either override those
choices (paternalism) or admit that life is not lexically prior in every
context. It cannot have both.

## T2: the self-reference argument

The paper's T2 says:

> "T2 (Self-Reference): Any argument against the primacy of life protection
> presupposes life protection." (`:3276`–`:3278`)

The argument is:

1. To argue against X, an agent must use decision-making capacity.
2. Decision-making capacity is what life protection protects.
3. Therefore, the argument presupposes life protection.

This is a genetic fallacy. The fact that an arguer uses a capacity does not show
that the capacity is the highest value. A mathematician uses arithmetic to argue
that arithmetic is not the highest value; the argument is not thereby
self-refuting. A person uses language to argue that language is not the highest
value; the argument is not thereby self-refuting. The use of a capacity is a
precondition for arguing, not a commitment to the capacity's supreme value.

The paper's analogy to Descartes' cogito is misleading. Descartes argues that
the act of doubting presupposes a doubter. That establishes the existence of a
doubter, not the supreme value of doubting. The paper establishes, at most, that
an arguer exists and has agency. It does not establish that agency is the
highest good.

## T3: the coordination argument

T3 says:

> "T3 (Coordination Necessity): For multiple life systems to coexist with
> conflicting values, they must coordinate on life protection." (`:3290`–`:3292`)

This is an empirical/game-theoretic claim, not a theorem. Cooperation can emerge
from many mechanisms:

- reciprocity and reputation;
- institutions and law;
- deterrence and balances of power;
- shared values other than life (honor, justice, faith, profit);
- interdependence and trade;
- communication and norms;
- exit and federalism.

The claim that life protection is the *only* stable coordination point is false
as stated. It may be a useful focal point in some games. It is not a universal
equilibrium result. The paper provides no game, payoffs, information structure,
or equilibrium analysis. It asserts the conclusion.

## The is-ought gap

The deepest problem is the is-ought gap. The paper's descriptive premises are:

- life is a precondition for value;
- agents use decision-making capacity;
- conflicting agents need coordination.

Its normative conclusion is:

- life ought to be the supreme decision constraint;
- life may never be traded for non-life objectives;
- any system that does not affirm this is unsafe.

No valid inference connects these without a normative premise. The paper
sometimes supplies one implicitly ("we must preserve life"), but it never
defends it. The "moral imperative" gate in the Foundational Sieve is asserted,
not argued.

This is not a knock-down objection to every moral argument. Normative premises
can be defended through reflective equilibrium, contractualism, capabilities,
natural law, or other methods. The point is that the paper does not do the work.
It treats the normative premise as self-evident because the descriptive premise
is dramatic.

## The "no life, no value" premise

The paper's key descriptive premise is:

> "Without life, no truth or value exists." (`:132`)

This is true on a valuer-dependent theory of value. It is false or incomplete on
several other views:

- **Moral realism:** moral facts may exist independently of valuers.
- **Aesthetic realism:** beauty may exist independently of perceivers.
- **Structural value:** ecosystems, mathematical structures, or possible
  futures may have value not reducible to actual valuers.
- **Theological value:** value may be grounded in God, not in finite agents.
- **Objective list theories:** some goods may be good for a being even if no
  one values them.

The paper's theological acknowledgements suggest a theological ground, which
makes the "no life, no value" premise even less secure: on many theological
views, value is grounded in God's nature, not in created life. The paper cannot
have it both ways.

Even if the premise is granted, it establishes that life is a *condition* of
value, not that life is the *highest* value. The paper needs a separate
argument for the ranking.

## What would a valid argument look like?

A valid argument would need something like:

> P1. In decisions that may cause catastrophic or irreversible harm to morally
>     considerable beings, avoiding that harm is a side-constraint.
> P2. A policy that ignores this constraint is unsafe.
> C. Therefore, catastrophic-harm avoidance should be a constraint on
>    optimization.

This argument is defensible, but it is narrower than the paper's claim. It is a
threshold principle, not a universal lexicographic rule. It applies to
catastrophic/irreversible harm, not to every decision that affects life. It
does not imply that life may never be traded for non-life objectives. It does
not imply that dissent is betrayal.

## Verdict

The paper confuses necessity with priority. T1 is valid but insufficient. T2 is
a genetic fallacy. T3 is an unsupported empirical claim. The is-ought gap is
never bridged. The central normative premise is asserted, not argued.

## Repair

1. State the normative premise explicitly.
2. Defend it against the strongest rival views.
3. Distinguish necessity, centrality, and lexical priority.
4. Replace "life may never be traded" with a threshold/proportionality rule.
5. Address the counterexamples: risky rescue, medical trade-offs, self-sacrifice,
   autonomy, justice, truth, and equality.
