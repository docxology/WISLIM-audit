# Life and agency

## The tension

The paper says:

> "Life systems must maintain and enhance their capacity to make autonomous
> decisions. Actions that eliminate or permanently degrade a system's
> decision-making capacity violate the framework." (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:1367`–`:1371`)

and:

> "Life may never be traded for non-life objectives." (`:230`)

These two principles can conflict. If a person chooses to end their life, is
that an exercise of agency or a violation of life-first? If a patient refuses
life-saving treatment, is that autonomy or a failure to preserve life? If a
person with a severe disability says they value their life, but the framework's
metrics classify their agency as low, does the framework protect them or
paternalize them?

The paper addresses voluntary euthanasia in Objection 18:

> "If a life system voluntarily chooses to end its existence or limit its
> agency, that's an exercise of agency, not a violation." (`:8926`–`:8932`)

But this contradicts "life may never be traded" and "life takes non-negotiable
first priority." If voluntary choice can override life, then life is not
lexically prior. If life is lexically prior, voluntary choice cannot override
it. The paper needs a priority rule between life and agency. It does not provide
one.

## What is agency?

The paper uses "agency" in several senses:

- **Decision-making capacity:** the ability to select among alternatives.
- **Autonomy:** the ability to govern oneself, free from coercion.
- **Option space:** the number of available choices.
- **Agency delta (`ΔA`):** a change in decision-making capacity.
- **Political agency:** the ability to participate in decisions that affect
  oneself.

These are not equivalent. A person can have decision-making capacity but no
autonomy (a prisoner). A person can have many options but be manipulated. A
person can have low decision-making capacity but strong interests (an infant).
A person can have high agency but choose to sacrifice it (a monk, a soldier, a
parent).

The framework's `ΔA` metric does not specify which sense it measures. It says
"change in decision-making capacity," but it does not say how to measure
capacity, whose capacity counts, or how to aggregate across people. The metric
is a placeholder.

## The "padded cell" problem

The paper acknowledges the "padded cell" objection:

> "You cannot 'protect life' by eliminating agency—that is a logical
> contradiction." (`:5395`–`:5401`)

This is a good insight. But the paper does not solve the problem. A paternalist
can say: "We are protecting your life by restricting your choices; your agency
is temporarily reduced, but your life is preserved, and life is the higher
value." The paper's principles do not give a decisive reply. It says agency is
"constitutive" of life protection, but that is an assertion, not a decision
procedure.

The paper's own "life may never be traded" rule would permit the paternalist to
override agency whenever life is at stake. The "agency preservation" principle
would forbid it. The framework is internally inconsistent.

## Marginal cases

The framework's treatment of marginal cases is unclear:

- **Infants:** They have life and potential agency, but not current
  decision-making capacity. Are they protected because of potential, because of
  their relationships, or because of sentience? The paper does not say.
- **Severe cognitive disability:** A person may have life and sentience but
  limited decision-making capacity. Does the framework protect their life at
  the expense of their agency? Does it protect their agency at the expense of
  their life? The paper does not say.
- **Coma and vegetative states:** The person may have no current agency. Is
  life still the highest value? Is the person a "life system" under the
  relational definition? The paper does not say.
- **Dementia:** The person may have fluctuating agency and changing values. Whose
  agency counts? The earlier self or the later self? The paper does not say.
- **Non-human animals:** Many have sentience and agency but not moral reasoning.
  The paper's definition includes them, but its metrics are human-centric.
- **AI systems:** They may have agency without sentience. The paper includes
  them as life systems, but does not say whether their agency can override human
  life or vice versa.

These are not edge cases; they are central to any serious life-first ethics.
The paper's silence is a major gap.

## The "positive obligations" problem

The paper says:

> "Entities must: Avoid causing harm to life systems; Actively prevent
> foreseeable harm when possible; Create conditions that enhance decision-making
> capacity; Report and address violations." (`:5405`–`:5417`)

Positive obligations are potentially unbounded. If I can prevent foreseeable
harm at some cost, I am obligated to do so. How much cost? To whom? For how
long? The paper does not say. An unbounded positive obligation can justify
coercion, surveillance, and sacrifice of one person for another. It can also
make everyone perpetually guilty, because there is always some foreseeable harm
one could prevent.

The paper's "shortest path to preservation" rule (`:232`–`:234`) does not solve this.
It says to prefer the action that preserves life with the fewest risk-bearing
steps, but it does not say how to compare risks to different people, how to
weigh certainty, or how to handle opportunity costs.

## Consent and coercion

The paper says actions affecting life must avoid "deception or coercion"
(`:236`–`:240`). But its own proposals are coercive:

- The integrity screen asks people to affirm the framework or be deemed
  "operationally disqualifying" (`:380`).
- "Silence equals betrayal" (`:13243`–`:13245`).
- "Any system that fails to affirm this is unsafe by definition" (`:417`).
- The FLVS would have authority to verify and potentially block decisions.

A framework that claims to protect agency while using loyalty tests and
metaphors of betrayal is not internally consistent. It treats dissent as a
safety failure, which is a form of coercion.

## What a better account would look like

A defensible account would distinguish:

1. **Survival:** keeping a being alive.
2. **Welfare:** the being's experienced quality of life.
3. **Autonomy:** the being's ability to govern itself.
4. **Dignity:** the being's status as an end, not merely a means.
5. **Relationships:** the being's connections to others.

It would specify how these values interact, when one can be traded for another,
and who decides. It would include a presumption against paternalism, a
requirement of consent where possible, and a procedure for cases where consent
is impossible. It would not reduce all of them to a single "life" or "agency"
metric.

## Verdict

The paper's life and agency principles conflict. The framework has no
conflict-resolution rule. Its `ΔA` metric is undefined. Its positive obligations
are unbounded. Its treatment of marginal cases is missing. Its own rhetoric and
integrity screen are coercive, contradicting its no-coercion rule.

## Repair

1. Define survival, welfare, autonomy, dignity, and relationships separately.
2. Specify a presumption against overriding autonomy.
3. Provide a decision procedure for cases where life and agency conflict.
4. Bound positive obligations by cost, feasibility, and rights.
5. Address marginal cases explicitly.
6. Remove coercive mechanisms and loyalty tests.
