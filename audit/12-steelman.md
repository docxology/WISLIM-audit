# The strongest version of the argument

## Why steelman?

The paper's own formulation is vulnerable to easy objections: the slogan is
ambiguous, the formula is ill-defined, the consensus is methodologically weak.
A fair audit should ask whether a stronger version of the argument survives.
This section reconstructs the best case for the paper's central intuition and
then states what it can and cannot support.

The strongest version is not "life is the most important truth in every
context." It is a constrained, threshold-based principle:

> **Catastrophic-harm side-constraint (CHSC).** In decisions that may cause
> catastrophic or irreversible harm to morally considerable beings, avoiding
> that harm functions as a side-constraint on optimization, not as one
> preference among many. The constraint can be overridden only by a comparable
> catastrophic harm, and only through a procedure that is transparent,
> accountable, and open to appeal.

This is a serious principle. It captures the paper's moral urgency without
claiming that life is lexically prior in every decision. It is compatible with
many ethical frameworks: consequentialism (as a constraint on catastrophic
risk), deontology (as a side-constraint), contractualism (as a term no one could
reasonably reject), care ethics (as attention to vulnerability), and
precautionary reasoning (as a response to uncertainty).

## The strongest premises

The steelman rests on five premises.

### P1. Morally considerable beings can be harmed catastrophically

Some harms are not merely bad; they are irreversible or  catastrophic. Death,
extinction, severe cognitive damage, and the destruction of the conditions for
flourishing are examples. These harms are not commensurable with ordinary
benefits. A policy that risks them for a modest gain is not just suboptimal; it
is reckless.

This premise is widely shared. It does not require a commitment to life-first.
It requires only that some harms are severe enough to constrain optimization.

### P2. Decision-makers are biased toward present, visible, and quantifiable goods

Human institutions and AI systems tend to optimize what they can measure. They
underweight diffuse, delayed, and uncertain harms. They also face pressure to
produce short-term results. This creates a systematic risk of catastrophic
harm even when no one intends it.

This is an empirical premise, supported by research on disaster prevention,
existential risk, and organizational failure. The paper's examples of
atrocity, environmental collapse, and AI risk are relevant here, even if the
paper does not cite the evidence carefully.

### P3. Some decisions are made under conditions of deep uncertainty

For many high-stakes decisions, the probabilities are unknown or contested. The
decision-maker cannot rely on expected utility maximization because the
probabilities and utilities are not well-defined. In these cases, a
precautionary side-constraint is a rational response to uncertainty.

This premise supports a threshold rule, not a lexical rule. It says: when the
downside is catastrophic and uncertain, do not treat it as a ordinary cost.
It does not say: never take any risk.

### P4. Coordination among pluralistic agents requires shared constraints

Agents with different values can coexist only if they share some constraints on
how they pursue those values. Without constraints, each agent has an incentive
to eliminate or dominate the others. A shared commitment to avoid catastrophic
harm can be a stable focal point, even when agents disagree about the good.

This is a game-theoretic and political premise. It is plausible in some
conditions, not all. It supports a modus vivendi, not a comprehensive moral
doctrine. It also requires institutions to monitor and enforce the constraints.

### P5. A constraint must be institutionalized to be effective

A moral principle without institutions is a hope. To be effective, the
constraint needs:

- clear rules;
- monitoring and verification;
- enforcement and sanctions;
- due process and appeal;
- independent oversight;
- protection for dissent and whistleblowers;
- adaptation and review.

This premise is the paper's best insight. Its governance proposals are an
attempt to institutionalize the constraint. The attempt is flawed, but the
insight is right: moral urgency without institutional design is empty.

## The strongest conclusion

From P1–P5, the strongest conclusion is:

> **C1.** In high-stakes decisions, institutions should adopt a
> catastrophic-harm side-constraint, with transparent rules, independent
> verification, and robust appeal rights.

This conclusion is defensible. It is also much narrower than the paper's
conclusion. It does not imply:

- that life is the highest value in every decision;
- that life may never be traded for non-life objectives;
- that all life systems have equal moral status;
- that a global life-first authority is legitimate;
- that dissent is betrayal;
- that the paper's formula, stress tests, or AI consensus prove the thesis.

## What the steelman preserves

The steelman preserves the paper's moral core:

- concern for preventable suffering;
- the intuition that some harms are too severe to be treated as ordinary costs;
- the importance of coordination under pluralism;
- the need for institutions that resist capture;
- the value of precaution under uncertainty;
- the idea that moral principles must be operationalized.

These are important contributions. The audit does not dismiss them. It argues
that the paper's strongest insights are embedded in an overclaiming framework
that undermines them.

## What the steelman cannot preserve

The steelman cannot preserve:

- **The lexical priority of life.** A threshold constraint is not a lexical
  rule. It permits trade-offs among lives and between life and other values in
  some cases.
- **The universal scope.** The constraint applies to catastrophic or
  irreversible harm, not to every decision that affects life.
- **The metaphysical claim.** The steelman does not need to claim that life is
  the foundation of all value. It needs only the weaker claim that some harms
  are severe enough to constrain optimization.
- **The epistemic claim.** The steelman does not rely on AI consensus. It relies
  on moral argument and empirical evidence about institutions.
- **The governance claim.** The steelman does not justify a global authority. It
  justifies piecemeal institutional reforms, tested in low-stakes domains.

## The strongest objection to the steelman

The strongest objection is that a catastrophic-harm side-constraint is too
vague to guide action. What counts as catastrophic? Who decides? How do we
compare catastrophic harms? Does the constraint forbid all risky innovation?
Does it paralyze decision-making?

These are serious objections. A defensible version would need:

- a threshold definition (e.g., extinction, irreversible loss of moral
  capacity, collapse of civilization);
- a procedure for classifying harms;
- a rule for comparing catastrophic harms;
- an exception for cases where inaction is itself catastrophic;
- a sunset and review mechanism;
- a burden of proof that is high but not impossible.

The paper does not provide these. Its formula and stress tests do not fill the
gap. But the gap is fillable. A research program could develop a rigorous
version of CHSC.

## The strongest version of the paper's title

If the paper wants to keep its title, the strongest interpretation is:

> "Life is most important" means that the avoidance of catastrophic harm to
> morally considerable beings is a side-constraint on all other goals, not that
> life is a lexical value that overrides every other consideration.

This interpretation is defensible. It is also a significant revision of the
paper's stated thesis. It would require the author to:

- abandon the claim that life may never be traded;
- abandon the "unsafe by definition" and "betrayal" rhetoric;
- abandon the AI-consensus proof;
- abandon the global governance blueprint;
- adopt a threshold rule with transparent institutions.

The audit recommends this revision.

## What the paper gets right

The paper is right that:

- preventable suffering is a serious moral problem;
- some harms are too severe to be treated as ordinary costs;
- coordination among pluralistic agents is difficult;
- institutions can fail catastrophically;
- AI systems need robust safety constraints;
- moral principles need operationalization;
- capture and gaming are real risks;
- dissent and error correction are essential.

These insights are worth preserving. The audit's verdict is "revise, do not
adopt" because the paper's insights are real but its strong claims are not
established.

## Verdict

The strongest version of the paper is a catastrophic-harm side-constraint, not
a lexical life-first rule. That version is defensible and worth developing. It
does not support the paper's title, formula, consensus claim, or governance
blueprint. The audit recommends separating the defensible core from the
overclaiming superstructure.
