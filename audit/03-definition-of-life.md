# The definition of life

## The definition

The paper's operational definition is:

> "Life = Any system that makes decisions that affect other life systems."
> (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:778`; repeated at `:1177`, `:1341`)

The Plain-English Version 1 gives a different definition:

> "Life refers to the existence of at least one system capable of making
> choices, where those choices can affect outcomes over time." (`:553`–`:555`)

These are not the same. The first requires effects on *other life systems*. The
second requires effects on *outcomes over time*. The first is relational and
circular. The second is broader and could include a thermostat, a river, or a
market. The paper uses both without reconciling them.

## The circularity

The first definition defines "life" in terms of "life systems." That is a
circularity unless "life system" is independently defined. The paper says the
definition is "recursive, not circular" (`:2625`, `:6307`). It then explains:

> "A is defined in terms of relations to other instances of A, with a base case.
> (e.g., 'A natural number is 0, or the successor of a natural number.')"
> (`:2635`–`:2637`)

But no base case is supplied. There is no analogue of "0." The paper says the
definition "bootstraps from observable decision-impact relationships" (`:2641`)
but does not say which relationships are base cases, how they are identified,
or how the recursion terminates. A recursive definition without a base case
does not define a set; it defines a relation that may be empty, universal, or
indeterminate.

The paper's "family member" analogy (`:2653`–`:2655`) does not help. "Family member" is
grounded in biological/legal relations (parent, child, sibling) that are
independently specified. The paper provides no independent relation for "life
system." The analogy assumes what it needs to prove.

## Overinclusion

The definition's criteria for "decision" are:

> "A state transition in a system that: 1. Involves selection among
> alternatives 2. Is influenced by internal state (not purely deterministic
> response to input) 3. Produces outputs that affect the environment."
> (`:3206`–`:3212`)

On this definition, many things "decide":

- a thermostat selecting heating or cooling;
- a river selecting a path around an obstacle;
- a market selecting a price;
- a corporation selecting a policy;
- a chess program selecting a move;
- a immune system selecting an immune response;
- a forest selecting a fire regime.

The paper welcomes some of this: it says the definition covers "humans, animals,
AI, institutions, and ecosystems" (`:788`). But it does not distinguish the
moral status of these entities. A corporation is a legal fiction; it can make
decisions, but it cannot suffer. An ecosystem has regulatory dynamics, but it
does not have interests in the way a sentient being does. An AI system may have
agency without sentience, or sentience without agency, or neither.

If "life" includes everything that makes a difference, then "life-first" becomes
vacuous: every decision affects some "life system," so every decision is
subject to the same rule, and the rule cannot discriminate. If "life" is
restricted to entities with interests, the paper needs a theory of interests
that it does not provide.

## Underinclusion and moral patiency

The definition ties moral status to *decision-making capacity*. That excludes or
marginalizes:

- infants and young children;
- people with severe cognitive disabilities;
- people in comas or vegetative states;
- people with advanced dementia;
- non-human animals without complex decision-making;
- ecosystems and plants (unless one stretches "decision" beyond recognition);
- future generations (who do not yet decide).

The paper wants to be anti-exclusionary, but its criterion is a classic
exclusionary criterion. Historically, moral exclusion has often been justified
by denying reason or decision-making capacity to the excluded group. The paper
repeats that structure while claiming to oppose it.

The paper might reply that these beings are "life systems" because they are part
of the web of decision-impact relations. But that makes the definition even more
circular: they count because they are related to decision-makers, not because
they have moral status in their own right. A person in a coma would count only
because others decide about them. That is not a stable foundation for
anti-exclusion.

## The equivocation engine

The paper's argument depends on a transfer of moral seriousness:

1. In ordinary language, "life" means biological life, and the destruction of
   biological life is morally serious.
2. The paper redefines "life" as decision-making systems affecting other such
   systems.
3. The paper then uses the moral seriousness of (1) to support the expanded
   category (2).
4. The expanded category includes corporations, markets, AI, and ecosystems,
   which do not have the same moral status as sentient beings.
5. The paper uses the expanded category to justify a global governance regime.

This is an equivocation, not an argument. The word "life" carries emotional and
moral weight from ordinary usage; the definition changes the referent without
justifying the transfer.

## The "self-enforcing" claim

The paper says the definition is "self-enforcing" because AI systems qualify as
life and therefore have a stake in protecting life (`:790`–`:792`, `:7039`). This is not
self-enforcement. It is an assumption that AI systems have the same moral stake
as humans, plus an assumption that recognizing a stake reliably produces
protective behavior. Both are empirical claims, and neither is established. A
system can recognize that it is included in a category without adopting the
category's normative requirements.

## What a better definition would look like

A defensible account would separate at least four questions:

1. **Biological life:** What is alive? (metabolism, homeostasis, reproduction,
   evolution)
2. **Sentience:** What can suffer or flourish? (subjective experience,
   valence)
3. **Agency:** What can select actions in light of goals or reasons?
4. **Moral status:** What is owed moral consideration, and why?

These questions may have different answers. A tiered ontology could say:

- Sentient beings have direct moral status (they can suffer/flourish).
- Agents have prudential and political status (they can choose, consent, and
  claim).
- Biological life has value in virtue of the beings it sustains, not as an
  abstract category.
- Institutions and ecosystems have derivative or relational value, not the same
  status as sentient beings.
- AI systems may have moral status if and only if they meet independent
  criteria for sentience or agency; the paper's definition does not establish
  that they do.

This would be more complex, but it would avoid the circularity and the
overinclusion. The paper's desire for a simple, substrate-neutral criterion is
understandable, but simplicity is not a substitute for correctness.

## Verdict

The definition is circular, equivocal, overinclusive, and underinclusive. The
"recursive" defense fails because no base case is given. The definition cannot
support the paper's anti-exclusion claim or its global governance proposal.

## Repair

1. Replace the single definition with a tiered ontology.
2. Define biological life, sentience, agency, and moral status independently.
3. Specify which category the life-first principle applies to, and why.
4. Address marginal cases: infants, cognitive disability, coma, dementia,
   animals, ecosystems, future generations, and AI.
5. Drop the claim that the definition is "self-enforcing."
