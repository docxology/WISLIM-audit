The list is entirely AI alignment and existential risk. It contains no work in
normative ethics, metaethics, political philosophy, moral status, population
ethics, decision theory under uncertainty, law, or governance.

## What the citations actually support

Each citation supports a narrow point:

- Russell & Norvig is a textbook. It supports general claims about AI.
- *Human Compatible* argues that AI systems should be uncertain about human
  preferences. It does not argue that life is the supreme decision constraint.
- Christiano et al. is about amplifying weak experts. It does not support
  life-first.
- Leike et al. is about reward modeling. It does not support life-first.
- Yudkowsky and Bostrom are about existential risk from advanced AI. They
  support the importance of safety, not the life-first hierarchy.
- Hadfield-Menell et al. is about cooperative inverse reinforcement learning. It
  does not support life-first.
- Friston's free-energy principle is a theory of brain function. It does not
  support a moral hierarchy.
- Ord is about existential risk. It supports precaution, not lexical priority.
- O'Keefe et al. is about cooperation among AI agents. It does not support
  life-first.

None of these works argues for the paper's central thesis. They are cited as
background, not as premises. The paper does not discuss their arguments,
limitations, or disagreements. It lists them and moves on.

## The decorative citation pattern

A decorative citation is a reference that signals erudition without doing
argumentative work. The pattern here is:

1. Name a well-known work.
2. Summarize it in one sentence.
3. Assert that the paper's framework "resolves" or "grounds" its problem.
4. Do not engage its actual arguments.

Examples:

> "Russell (2019) identifies the core failure of current systems as their lack of
> uncertainty about human preferences. The Life-First framework addresses this
> by making the preservation of life the explicit, non-negotiable ground of all
> decision-making." (`:278`–`:286`)

This does not follow. Russell's point is that AI systems should be uncertain
about what humans want. The paper's framework is a substantive moral hierarchy
that claims to know what humans want (life first). It does not resolve Russell's
problem; it sidesteps it by asserting a preference.

> "Christiano et al. (2018) ... The Life-First framework provides the
> meta-principle that makes such approaches safe." (`:284`–`:290`)

No argument is given for why life-first is the meta-principle, or why
amplification needs it.

> "Friston (2010) ... The Life-First framework extends this logic to ethics."
> (`:298`–`:304`)

Extending a descriptive theory of brain function to ethics is a category move,
not an extension. The paper does not justify it.

## What is missing

A serious treatment of life-first ethics would engage:

- **Moral status:** Singer, Regan, Nussbaum, Jaworska, McMahan.
- **Consequentialism and its limits:** Mill, Sidgwick, Parfit, Williams,
  Smart.
- **Deontology:** Kant, Korsgaard, O'Neill, Scanlon.
- **Virtue ethics:** Aristotle, Foot, MacIntyre, Hursthouse.
- **Care ethics:** Gilligan, Noddings, Held, Tronto.
- **Contractualism:** Rawls, Scanlon, Gauthier.
- **Capabilities:** Sen, Nussbaum.
- **Population ethics:** Parfit, Arrhenius, Broome.
- **Risk and precaution:** Jonas, Sunstein, Gardiner, Ord.
- **Paternalism and autonomy:** Mill, Dworkin, Feinberg, Quong.
- **Legitimacy and governance:** Waldron, Buchanan, Rawls, Habermas.
- **AI safety:** Amodei et al., Hendrycks et al., Hubinger et al., Soares
  et al.
- **Animal ethics:** Singer, Regan, Francione, Gruen.
- **Environmental ethics:** Leopold, Naess, Rolston, Jamieson.

The paper cites none of these. It claims to resolve "centuries of debate"
(`:1231`) without engaging the debate. That is not scholarship; it is a
declaration.

## The "no legitimate rebuttal" claim

The paper says:

> "No legitimate rebuttal has ever been presented." (`:13229`)

This is an empirical claim about the literature. It is false on its face. There
are many rebuttals to the general idea that life is the supreme value:

- consequentialist arguments that trade lives for other goods;
- deontological arguments that constrain how we may use people;
- contractualist arguments that ground morality in mutual justification;
- virtue arguments that the good life is not mere survival;
- care arguments that relationships, not bare life, are central;
- political arguments that a global life-first authority would be illegitimate;
- existential risk arguments that some values may be worth risking life for;
- religious arguments that martyrdom or sacrifice can be meaningful.

The paper does not engage any of them. It asserts that no legitimate rebuttal
exists and defines dissent as betrayal. That is not a literature review.

## The AI letters as sources

Appendix B is not a citation of external research. It is a set of letters
produced for the paper. The letters are not peer-reviewed, not independently
elicited, and not accompanied by prompts or transcripts. They cannot bear the
weight the paper places on them. See `audit/07-consensus-and-evidence.md`.

## The publication record

The public record has three inconsistent labels:

- **PhilPapers:** "8th edition."
- **PDF:** Version 2.0.1, dated 4 February 2026 (`:25`).
- **PubPub:** Release #1, dated 4 February 2026.

The paper does not explain the relationship between the labels. A reader cannot
tell whether the 8th edition is the same text, a revision, or a different work.
The audit treats the PDF as the object and records the inconsistency.

The DOI is `10.21428/4831231e.8c4c6c8f`. The PhilPapers ID is `WISLIM`.

## The license ambiguity

PubPub states CC-BY 4.0. The PDF closes with:

> "I dedicate this work to the public domain." (`:13255`)

These are not the same. CC-BY 4.0 requires attribution; a public-domain
dedication does not. The paper does not reconcile them. Until the author
clarifies, the audit treats the license as unresolved, preserves full
attribution, and does not relicense the source. See `evidence/provenance.md`.

## The missing engagement with critics

The paper's "Observed Reception" section describes critics as evasive:

> "A recurring pattern has been observed: acknowledgment without engagement,
> deflection to side-issues (tone, wording, politics), requests to 'hold,' and
> prolonged silence without a stated rebuttal." (`:342`–`:344`)

This is not a citation audit. It is an anecdotal complaint. It does not name
the critics, the contexts, or the responses. It treats non-engagement as
evidence of the paper's truth. That is the opposite of scholarly engagement.

## What a serious literature review would look like

1. State the thesis precisely.
2. Identify the main positions in normative ethics and metaethics.
3. Explain how each position bears on the thesis.
4. Cite the strongest objections and respond to them.
5. Distinguish agreement on the importance of life from agreement on the
   life-first hierarchy.
6. Acknowledge unresolved disagreements.
7. Avoid claiming that no legitimate rebuttal exists.

The paper does none of this. Its bibliography is a gesture toward AI alignment,
not an engagement with ethics.

## Verdict

The citations are decorative. They support general AI safety points, not the
life-first thesis. The paper ignores the ethics literature it claims to resolve.
Its "no legitimate rebuttal" claim is false. The publication labels and license
are inconsistent.

## Repair

1. Add a substantive literature review.
2. Engage the strongest objections from consequentialism, deontology, virtue
   ethics, care ethics, contractualism, and political philosophy.
3. Cite sources for empirical claims.
4. Reconcile the edition labels and license.
5. Remove "no legitimate rebuttal" and describe the actual state of the debate.
