## The premise and the priority rule

The paper calls life “the necessary precondition for any agent to experience,
value, judge, prove, or act on anything at all” and then says that “whenever
life is affected, life takes non-negotiable first priority over all non-life
objectives” [@wishengrad2026] (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:150–154`). The first statement concerns
what makes valuation possible. The second gives an ordering for decisions
when objectives compete. A rule assigning overriding priority to every
necessary condition would be a further normative premise. The audit
finds that the cited passage does not defend such a rule [@wislim_audit].

This inference also depends on what “life” picks out. The plain-English version
describes a system able to make choices that affect outcomes. The operational
definition instead speaks of systems whose decisions affect “other life
systems” [@wishengrad2026] (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:553–555 and 778`). The audit asks both what
grounds the definition of a life system and how a capacity to affect outcomes
would establish moral standing. Treating the definitions as interchangeable
leaves those questions open [@wislim_audit].

The formula at `source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:431` assigns a product of two factors
divided by a third. Setting the life factor to zero yields zero when the
denominator is positive, as the source’s proof assumes
[@wishengrad2026] (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:644–652`). The algebra follows from the expression.
A normative conclusion would require an additional argument that the
variables and the resulting order represent the ethical judgment under
discussion. Likewise, approaching a zero denominator gives a mathematical
limit whose ethical interpretation requires separate support [@wishengrad2026; @wislim_audit] (`source/WISLIM-v2.0.1.txt:465–467`).
