## Review claim

> explicitly abstracting away the necessity of biological life or a living valuer
>
> — `reviews/oph-lfdi-57/source/LFDI_Alignment_Audit-57.verified.txt:2`

## Intrinsic assessment

The sentence is descriptively accurate if read as 'OPH's observer definition does not require biological life.' It becomes a fallacy if read as 'OPH denies the reality or value of life.' The review does not distinguish these readings, and the rest of the document relies on the stronger one. Not requiring a property is not removing it: a definition of 'vehicle' that does not require wheels takes no wheels off any car.

## Extrinsic assessment

The book lists 'Life. Obviously not' among the things an observer does not need, and says 'An observer here is any bounded system that keeps records.' The same passage says the arrival of living things 'introduced a much better one of a kind that had been there all along', and the chapter scores a person and a thermostat by one test. The flagship's wording is narrower than the review's: its operational observer is 'defined by access and record interfaces, without an additional claim about consciousness', and it 'needs an additional account before any claim about phenomenal qualities follows'. 'Biological', 'life' and 'valuer' do not occur in the flagship source at either release, and 'living' occurs only in the journal title 'Living Reviews in Relativity'. The descriptive point is fair; 'explicitly' fits the book, not the flagship; the later inference that OPH 'subordinates Life' is not supported.

## Summary

OPH does define observers without requiring life. That is a scope decision about physics, not a denial that living agents exist or matter: the book places living things inside the observer class.

## Suggested response

Grant the descriptive point: OPH's physics does not require biological observers. Separate that from the moral claim that life is worthless or that OPH denies life's value.
