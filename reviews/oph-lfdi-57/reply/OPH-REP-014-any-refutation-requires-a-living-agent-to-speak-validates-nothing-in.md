## Summary

Speaking shows that a speaker exists. Denying that life outranks everything does not deny that a speaker exists, so the denial contradicts nothing it presupposes. The same form would crown oxygen or grammar, and it leaves the claim unable to lose.

## Assessment

What the performance shows: Hintikka (1962) reads the cogito as a performance, in which an utterance can show that its utterer exists. No one in this exchange denies that. The response's author has written the point down himself: '[we have to exist for this exclaimation’s validity]'.

What a denial presupposes: a performative contradiction needs the utterance to presuppose what it denies. Someone who says that life is not the supreme constraint presupposes a speaker. They do not presuppose that life is the supreme constraint, or that E∞ = (L × U) / D. The denial and its presupposition are different propositions, so no contradiction arises. A necessary condition is not a lexical priority, which WISLIM-008, WISLIM-009, and WISLIM-010 track.

As evidence: that a living agent spoke is certain whether the ranking is right or wrong. An observation certain under both hypotheses has a likelihood ratio of one, and it supports neither.

It proves too much: a refutation also requires language, oxygen, and logic. The same form would crown each as the most important truth.

It seals itself: if every attempted disproof counts as proof, no observation could count against the claim, and calling it 'unrefuted' then reports nothing about its strength. WISLIM's own version is 'To argue against it is to rely on it'.

The argument form has a serious literature, which is the place to take it. Apel (1975) pursues ultimate grounding this way. Habermas (1990) uses performative contradiction without accepting ultimate grounding. Stroud (1968) objects that conditions of this kind may show only what we must believe. None of these treats the move as ending the discussion.

**Kind of claim:** `logic`: the inference does not follow as stated.
